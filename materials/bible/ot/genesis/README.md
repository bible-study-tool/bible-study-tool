# Historical Preamble — The Book of Genesis

## Book Overview

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Author** | Moses (Mosaic authorship affirmed by SDA hermeneutics, Jesus in John 5:46–47, and the 1986 "Methods of Bible Study") |
| **Date of writing** | Circa 1450–1400 BC (during or shortly after the Exodus and wilderness period) |
| **Original audience** | The nation of Israel at the foot of Sinai — recently freed slaves being constituted as God's covenant people |
| **Language** | Biblical Hebrew (with occasional Aramaic loanwords) |
| **Literary genre** | Theological history and origin narrative (*toledot* structure: "These are the generations of...") |
| **Central purpose** | To establish Israel's identity, calling, and covenant relationship with the God who created the heavens and the earth — and to ground all of redemptive history in the promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob |

## Historical Era

Genesis covers a vast span — from **Creation** through the **Patriarchal Period** (circa 2100–1800 BC). Moses compiled and wrote it during the **Late Bronze Age** (circa 1450–1200 BC). Key historical anchors:

| Narrative | Approximate Date | Historical Context |
|---|---|---|
| Creation & Fall | Undated (primeval history) | Before recorded human civilization |
| Flood & Noah | ~3000–2500 BC (est.) | Contemporaneous with earliest Mesopotamian civilizations |
| Tower of Babel | ~2500–2200 BC (est.) | Early urban cultures (Sumer, Akkad) |
| Abraham's call | ~2100–2000 BC | Middle Bronze Age; Ur was a sophisticated Sumerian city-state |
| Sodom & Gomorrah | ~2000–1900 BC | Cities of the plain (possibly Dead Sea region) |
| Joseph in Egypt | ~1900–1700 BC | Middle Kingdom Egypt (possibly Hyksos period) |

## Ancient Near Eastern (ANE) Context — Essential Background

Genesis was written *into* a world saturated with competing creation, flood, and origin stories. Moses's audience knew these narratives. Understanding the differences is critical for proper interpretation:

### Creation (Genesis 1–2) vs. ANE Cosmology
Mesopotamian creation texts (Enuma Elish) describe creation as the result of warfare between gods, with humanity created from a slain god's blood as slaves to serve the gods. Genesis 1 is a systematic, anti-mythological proclamation:
- **One God** — not a pantheon warring for supremacy
- **Creation by speech** — not by violence or sexual generation
- **Humanity as Image-bearers** (*imago Dei*) — not slaves; dignity is intrinsic
- **Sabbath rest** — God models rest; the seventh day is not an afterthought

### Flood (Genesis 6–9) vs. Gilgamesh Epic
The Gilgamesh flood narrative (Tablet XI) and the Atrahasis Epic share structural similarities with the biblical account. This does not imply dependence or mythologization — rather, a shared memory of a real global event that filtered through multiple cultural traditions. The biblical account differs decisively in its **moral framework**: Noah is saved because of righteousness and covenant, not because a sympathetic god wanted to preserve humanity from noisy gods trying to sleep.

### Covenant Structure vs. Suzerain-Vassal Treaties
The Abrahamic covenant (Genesis 12, 15, 17) and later the Mosaic covenant follow the structure of **Hittite suzerain-vassal treaties** of the second millennium BC — identifying the sovereign (God), rehearsing the history of benevolent acts, and laying out obligations and blessings/curses. Recognizing this structure clarifies why Abraham "cut" the covenant in Genesis 15 (the ritual of passing between halved animals) and what it meant for God alone to pass between them.

## Geographical Context

| Location | Significance |
|---|---|
| **Eden** (exact location debated) | East of Israel; four rivers including Tigris and Euphrates — Mesopotamian heartland |
| **Ur of the Chaldees** | Sophisticated Sumerian city on the Euphrates; Abraham's starting point (Genesis 11:31) |
| **Haran** | Trading crossroads in upper Mesopotamia; Abraham's transitional home |
| **Canaan** | The Promised Land; territory of multiple city-states and peoples; strategically between Egypt and Mesopotamia |

| **Beersheba** | Southern border of Canaan; patriarchal covenant site ("Well of the oath/seven") |
| **Shechem, Bethel, Hebron** | The patriarchal circuit — places of altar, prayer, and covenant renewal |

## Key Cultural Keys for Reading Genesis

1. **Primogeniture** (firstborn rights): In the ANE, the firstborn son inherited the family leadership, double portion, and covenant blessing. Genesis systematically *overturns* this pattern — Isaac over Ishmael, Jacob over Esau, Joseph and Ephraim over Manasseh — underscoring that God's election is by grace, not birth order.
2. **Levirate custom and clan law**: Marriage, inheritance, and land rights in Genesis operate within clan-based customary law that differs sharply from modern Western norms. Tamar's actions (Genesis 38) are incomprehensible without understanding levirate obligation.
3. **Hospitality as sacred obligation**: In the desert world, receiving a stranger, feeding them, and protecting them under your roof was a binding moral duty (cf. Abraham and the three visitors, Genesis 18; Lot, Genesis 19). Violation of hospitality was one of the gravest sins conceivable.
4. **Naming as prophetic declaration**: In Hebrew thought, a name was not a label but a declaration of character and destiny. Every naming event in Genesis is theologically significant: Eve (*hawwah*, "living"), Babel (*balal*, "confusion"), Abraham ("father of multitudes"), Israel ("he struggles with God").
5. **The *toledot* structure**: Genesis is organized by ten "these are the generations of" (*ʾēlleh tôlĕdōt*) headings, functioning as chapter markers: heavens and earth (2:4), Adam (5:1), Noah (6:9), sons of Noah (10:1), Shem (11:10), Terah (11:27), Ishmael (25:12), Isaac (25:19), Esau (36:1), Jacob (37:2). This is a formal literary structure, not an accidental arrangement.
